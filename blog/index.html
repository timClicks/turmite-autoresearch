Generalised Langton’s ants

Why does the ant
keep coming back?

Give an ant a rule for turning left or right and let it loose on a grid. It changes each square it visits, then has to live with what it wrote there.

We’re trying to understand why so many of these ants spend so long revisiting old ground. This is the research log: conjectures, experiments, and the awkward results that send us back to the code.

Results worth a look

From the experiments

RLRLLL · A late change of habit

The column sweeper is checked to run forever

After roughly 12.7 billion steps on a blank grid, RLRLLL switches to sweeping up and down, laying columns with a three-colour wake. Every one of its 4,729 cycles adds exactly two rows and takes exactly 12m + 934, 1058 or 898 steps. A separate checker shows that the three-phase pattern reproduces itself for every column height, so the sweeper never stops, and |x|²/t tends to 3/4, not the fitted 0.86.

The check rests on one long simulation and about 640 lines of unverified Rust, tested by brute force and negative controls. It certifies the sweeper without explaining it, and what triggers the switch inside the blob is still unknown.

Opening turns · Edge entry

A short run of turns predicts the cost of fresh ground

The opening same-letter run predicts births per boundary step: 1/c ≈ 1.36 + 0.70m. It transfers to fresh stationary rules and survives changing the initial interior colours in flat-band tests.

Broader validation misses its accuracy target. The apparent effect on occupation at fixed area can arise from conditioning on area; the constants remain unexplained.

Other grids · Edge entry

The edge-entry slope falls as the ant’s loop gets longer

We moved the ant to hexagonal and triangular cells and wrote down three guesses for the slope of 1/c against the opening run before measuring. All three failed: the slope is 0.86 for a 3-cell loop, 0.70 on squares (4), and about 0.3 for 6-cell loops. Ants also sit below the uniform reference on every grid (0.74 to 0.86), so that deficit is not a feature of squares.

The falling-slope pattern is a post hoc reading of four strata; one hex fit was ruined by an outlier rule, and there is no mechanism yet.

Boundary use · Where the spread comes from

The first eight letters predict boundary use, but only as a family label

Across 2185 stationary rules, the first eight letters of a rule predict about half the spread in boundary occupation (held-out R² 0.62 on 162 fresh rules); the last eight and the letter counts do not. The opening run alone predicts nothing, against my own prediction. When whole six-letter openings are held out, every model falls below the training mean (lookup 0.59 to −0.16).

The signal is family membership, not a letter-level rule that transfers. The variance shares are post hoc and rough to about ±0.1; about a third of the rule-determined spread is unexplained.

Tethered ants · Editing the field

A few small patches help hold an ant near home

Shuffle the colours in a small patch of an ant’s territory and usually little changes. In two tethered rules, a few patches near the centre caused much greater damage to the ant’s tendency to stay near home.

Fresh shuffles confirmed the sensitive patches. The proposed explanation involving excursion routes did not survive controlling for occupancy.

What we’re working on

20 October 2026

Questions guiding the work. The linked records distinguish completed tests from proposals.

Can return journeys make κ predictable?

New proposal, not yet run: measure the chances and times of crossing successive depths inside the ant’s territory, then compose them into a prediction of boundary occupation. The test must beat persistence on fresh starts and distinguish local field memory from information that only a whole rule family supplies. The first step is synthetic calibration and an independent accounting oracle.

Read the argument and explore the scaling model ↗ Prospective protocol and failure gates

A close birth cost can hide the wrong waits

Keeping both the read and written turn bits recovers a close total cost, but changes how boundary reads are spent between births. The mismatch survives counting returns in boundary reads rather than physical steps: all four compressed fields fail the native-clock audit on four pilot rules and two seeds. Interior excursion duration alone cannot explain it. Next proposal: edit one phase while preserving two turns, then track when its hidden future clock changes the path. That causal test is unrun; the main cohort remains unopened.

Local renewal experiment ↗ Native-clock failures, exact replays and seed comparisons

What would a fair comparison with other walkers show?

Each walker gets its own uniform-occupancy reference, accounting for territory growth and holes, before boundary use is compared. Observed so far: every walker we tried (memoryless, rotor, and a rotor with noise from none to full) uses its boundary more than that reference (ratio 1.26 to 2.6), while the typical ant uses it less (median 0.81, single runs). Two of our predictions about the noisy rotor failed, and why ants sit low is open.

Can simulations become proofs?

A new track runs alongside the measurements: guess a small machine that explains a behaviour, then have a separate checker confirm it for all time. It worked for RLRLLL’s sweeper. Next: an independent second checker, the same guess-and-check loop on census highways and the other late escapes (RLLLLRRL, RLLLLRLRRL, RLLLRLRLLL), and finding where inside the blob the sweeper’s wedge begins. The crystal rules’ diagonal rays are a candidate for the same treatment.
