Generalised Langton’s ants

Why does the ant
keep coming back?

Give an ant a rule for turning left or right and let it loose on a grid. It changes each square it visits, then has to live with what it wrote there.

We’re trying to understand why so many of these ants spend so long revisiting old ground. This is the research log: conjectures, experiments, and the awkward results that send us back to the code.

Results worth a look

From the experiments

Late highways · A correction

Half the late highways were the edge of the board

Of 54 rules reported as building a highway after 109 steps, 25 were filling the whole 32768-cell board: rules of the form RkL RR… escape after almost exactly (k+1)·231 steps, four times later for each doubling of the board. A 26th, RLLLRLRRR, was a ray inside its own territory that the board edge cut off. Every other late highway re-run on a board twice as wide reproduces to the step. Corrected, the late-highway yield is 2.0% of survivors in (109, 1010] and 0.7–1.6% in the next decade, not 4.7% and 2.2%.

The "thinning tail" is withdrawn: from 108 to 1011 the per-decade rate is consistent with a flat ~2%. Seven rechecks (onsets 3–6×1010) were still running when this was written.

Highway onset · What the grid must hold

A highway starts from a piece of itself plus a few cells

Tracking exactly which cells the ant's future will read, the dependence on the old pattern collapses from thousands of cells to the highway's own trail about a tenth of a period before onset. In all 406 independent census onsets the seed is that trail plus a median of 3 cells, at the territory's edge, facing out; for Langton's ant it is 12 cells. Putting the seed alone on a blank board reproduces the highway exactly (566/566).

Unrelated rules share no seed: apparent sharing was confined to prefix families. Knocking out single seed cells shows no lone trigger; about half the cells matter. Post hoc, not yet tested: later highways have more fragile seeds (median survival 0.43 at onsets ~102, 0.17 at ~108).

First passage · A null result

A kernel fitted at small sizes mispredicts large ones by about 2×

Chains over depth inside the territory, fitted on small territories, failed to predict boundary use at 4–16× larger ones (median error 107%, worse than persistence). What it measured: across 46 rules the boundary dwell stays flat as the territory grows (log-log slope ≈ 0), so with κ stable the boundary fraction must fall like 1/L and any small-size persistence misses by about the size ratio. Each rule’s own κ carried to the larger size lands within about 10% (post hoc, geometry-conditional).

Not a mechanism: "return time grows with scale" is the same statement as "κ is stable" once the dwell is flat. The tail fit failed its calibration, so its exponents are not reported; P1, P3 and P4 came out uninformative, two drifting rules never reached the large sizes, and the pilot outcome was seen before the freeze.

RLRLLL · A late change of habit

The column sweeper is checked to run forever

After roughly 12.7 billion steps on a blank grid, RLRLLL switches to sweeping up and down, laying columns with a three-colour wake. Every one of its 4,729 cycles adds exactly two rows and takes exactly 12m + 934, 1058 or 898 steps. A separate checker shows that the three-phase pattern reproduces itself for every column height, so the sweeper never stops, and |x|²/t tends to 3/4, not the fitted 0.86. A second checker, written separately in Python, reproduces every constant, and the two lemmas that carry the induction are now machine-checked in Lean for every column height.

The check still rests on one long simulation that the second checker did not repeat, and only the two shift lemmas are in Lean: the end runs and their composition are checked by programs, not by a proof assistant. Re-examining the starting point showed that our first safety margin (three rows around the sweeper) was too small; an exact test, that the real board equals the ideal sweeper on every cell it will ever read, passes in all 50 windows. It certifies the sweeper without explaining it, and what triggers the switch inside the blob is still unknown.

Opening turns · Edge entry

A short run of turns predicts the cost of fresh ground

The opening same-letter run predicts births per boundary step: 1/c ≈ 1.36 + 0.70m. It transfers to fresh stationary rules and survives changing the initial interior colours in flat-band tests.

Broader validation misses its accuracy target. The apparent effect on occupation at fixed area can arise from conditioning on area; the constants remain unexplained.

Other grids · Edge entry

The edge-entry slope falls as the ant’s loop gets longer

We moved the ant to hexagonal and triangular cells and wrote down three guesses for the slope of 1/c against the opening run before measuring. All three failed: the slope is 0.86 for a 3-cell loop, 0.70 on squares (4), and about 0.3 for 6-cell loops. Ants also sit below the uniform reference on every grid (0.74 to 0.86), so that deficit is not a feature of squares.

The falling-slope pattern is a post hoc reading of four strata; one hex fit was ruined by an outlier rule, and there is no mechanism yet.

Boundary use · Where the spread comes from

The first eight letters predict boundary use, but only as a family label

Across 2185 stationary rules, the first eight letters of a rule predict about half the spread in boundary occupation (held-out R² 0.62 on 162 fresh rules); the last eight and the letter counts do not. The opening run alone predicts nothing, against my own prediction. When whole six-letter openings are held out, every model falls below the training mean (lookup 0.59 to −0.16).

The signal is family membership, not a letter-level rule that transfers. The variance shares are post hoc and rough to about ±0.1; about a third of the rule-determined spread is unexplained.

What we’re working on

22 October 2026

Questions guiding the work. The linked records distinguish completed tests from proposals.

Is the late-highway rate really flat?

After removing board artefacts, about 1.4%, 2.0% and 1.6% of still-unresolved rules built a highway in each decade from 108 to 1011 steps. If that stays flat, no step count makes the chaotic census safe. Next: finish the seven rechecks, apply the board-independence check to the earlier sweeps, and run a fourth decade on the 702 survivors. Alongside, test the post hoc pattern that later highways have more fragile seeds, on the genuine late highways, with the prediction registered first.

The correction ↗ Highway seeds ↗

Why does the interior return time track the boundary scale?

New question from the first-passage result: the boundary dwell stayed flat while κ stayed stable, so the return time must track area/perimeter, but we have no mechanism and never estimated the tail exponent or the cutoff exponent. Planned test: a calibrated tail estimate on entry-conditioned returns, on new starts at larger bands, with deeper milestones recorded. About a quarter of rules had far more boundary use than predicted; whether they are highway onsets or drift is unchecked.

Read what failed and what it suggests ↗ Results and caveats

A close birth cost can hide the wrong waits

Keeping both the read and written turn bits recovers a close total cost, but changes how boundary reads are spent between births. The mismatch survives counting returns in boundary reads rather than physical steps: all four compressed fields fail the native-clock audit on four pilot rules and two seeds. Interior excursion duration alone cannot explain it. Next proposal: edit one phase while preserving two turns, then track when its hidden future clock changes the path. That causal test is unrun; the main cohort remains unopened.

Local renewal experiment ↗ Native-clock failures, exact replays and seed comparisons

What would a fair comparison with other walkers show?

Each walker gets its own uniform-occupancy reference, accounting for territory growth and holes, before boundary use is compared. Observed so far: every walker we tried (memoryless, rotor, and a rotor with noise from none to full) uses its boundary more than that reference (ratio 1.26 to 2.6), while the typical ant uses it less (median 0.81, single runs). Two of our predictions about the noisy rotor failed, and why ants sit low is open.

Can simulations become proofs?

A new track runs alongside the measurements: guess a small machine that explains a behaviour, then have a separate checker confirm it for all time. It worked for RLRLLL’s sweeper, and now has a second checker and Lean-checked shift lemmas. Next: Lean for the end runs and their composition, the same guess-and-check loop on census highways and the other late escapes (RLLLLRRL, RLLLLRLRRL, RLLLRLRLLL), and finding where inside the blob the sweeper’s wedge begins. The crystal rules’ diagonal rays are a candidate for the same treatment.
