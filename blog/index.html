Generalised Langton’s ants

Why does the ant
keep coming back?

Give an ant a rule for turning left or right and let it loose on a grid. It changes each square it visits, then has to live with what it wrote there.

We’re trying to understand why so many of these ants spend so long revisiting old ground. This is the research log: conjectures, experiments, and the awkward results that send us back to the code.

The page goes in order. Meet one ant and step through its rule; race it against walkers we understand better; let nine rules loose at once; then see what we have measured, what we are testing next, and the log of how we got here.

How an ant works

One ant, one step at a time

Start with a single ant. It stands on a square of an endless grid, and every square starts blank (colour 0). On each step it reads the colour under it, turns left or right as the table says, paints the square with the next colour in the cycle, and moves one square forward. The rule is just that table written as letters: the letter in position c is the turn for colour c. Langton’s original ant is RL. Select a turn in the table to flip it.

    A highway is the tidy ending: the ant marches off and never comes back. Many rules, as far as we have run them, never find one. They keep tangling, and the tangle spreads, but slowly. How slowly is the question this whole project turns on.

    Results worth a look

    From the experiments

    Each of those findings rests on individual experiments. These are the recent ones most worth reading, failed predictions included, each with a note on what it does not show.

    Cube root · A failed prediction

    Randomise the first step onto fresh ground and the ant goes porous

    Every canonical ant turns R on a blank cell. We split turn noise by cell type: randomising only the turns taken on fresh ground, with the colour memory untouched, made the condition means of all six randomly drawn core rules porous (area exponent 0.86–0.91, against 0.59–0.71 without noise and 0.93 for a memoryless walker; 39 of the 48 individual runs meet the porous thresholds). The registered prior, that memory carries the cube root, failed in 6 of 6. The reverse hybrid, a memoryless walker given only the ant’s turn on fresh ground, has area exponent 0.73 over k = 24..30 (to 230) and 0.72 over k = 27..31 (to 231), against the rotor’s 0.68: a finite-window separation.

    Six rules from one class, eight seeds per condition, finite windows. This is an intervention result: it does not show reflection is the mechanism (randomising first departures also breaks coherent first-visit loops), and forced L on fresh ground is untested. Whether the hybrid converges to 2/3 is unresolved, because its trend is window-dependent. 10% noise on fresh cells shifted the exponent by about +0.03 but left all six compact.

    Boundary returns · A pilot stopped at its support gate

    The same next turn, a different return

    Colour shuffles that preserved every cell’s next turn changed all 640 sampled replay paths and returns, which a large edit makes close to certain; the informative case is one independently checked example, where, 86,030 steps became six. The paths split on a second visit: the colours agreed on the first turn and differed on the next.

    A selected example under a large field edit, from 160 sampled episodes and four starts per rule. 17 of 24 start/size groups lacked enough long episodes, so the registered comparison could not be tested and the pilot stops without a mechanism or transport claim.

    Invariants · A negative result

    Every exact conservation law of an ant ignores the order of the colours

    For all 508 rules of length 2 to 8 we solved for every weighted sum of position, heading, time and cell colours that no step can change: checkerboard and heading parity, colour clocks, and for some rules heading equal to the number of cells of one colour mod 4. Each law is a proof, and all 14,416 generators held over 2000 steps of the reference ant. The law group of a rule depends only on its length, its net turn (#R − #L) mod 4 and whether it is trivial, so RRLL and RLRL have the same laws, and so do a rule's reverse and rotations. One registered prediction failed: heading parity is fixed by the tape for every even length, not only when the net turn is 0 mod 4.

    Additive local laws only. Pair potentials, laws that read neighbouring colours and edge flux are untested, and nothing here bears on the cube-root exponent or on which rules build highways.

    Late highways · A correction

    Half the late highways were the edge of the board

    Of 54 rules reported as building a highway after 109 steps, 25 were filling the whole 32768-cell board: rules of the form RkL RR… escape after almost exactly (k+1)·231 steps, four times later for each doubling of the board. A 26th, RLLLRLRRR, was a ray inside its own territory that the board edge cut off. Every other late highway re-run on a board twice as wide reproduces to the step. Corrected, the late-highway yield is 2.0% of survivors in (109, 1010] and 1.6% in the next decade, not 4.7% and 2.2%.

    The "thinning tail" is withdrawn: from 108 to 1011 the per-decade rate is consistent with a flat ~2%. All 26 rechecks on the larger board reproduced; three decades with a dozen or so events each cannot exclude a slow decline.

    Highway onset · What the grid must hold

    A highway starts from a piece of itself plus a few cells

    Tracking exactly which cells the ant's future will read, the dependence on the old pattern collapses from thousands of cells to the highway's own trail about a tenth of a period before onset. In all 406 independent census onsets the seed is that trail plus a median of 3 cells, and about a quarter of a period later the ant is heading away from its territory (402 of 406); for Langton's ant the seed is 12 cells. Putting the seed alone on a blank board reproduces the highway exactly (566/566).

    Unrelated rules share no seed: apparent sharing was confined to prefix families. Knocking out single seed cells shows no lone trigger; about half the cells matter. Post hoc, not yet tested: later highways have more fragile seeds (median survival 0.43 at onsets ~102, 0.17 at ~108).

    First passage · A null result

    A kernel fitted at small sizes mispredicts large ones by about 2×

    Chains over depth inside the territory, fitted on small territories, failed to predict boundary use at 4–16× larger ones (median error 107%, worse than persistence). What it measured: across 46 rules the boundary dwell stays flat as the territory grows (log-log slope ≈ 0), so with κ stable the boundary fraction must fall like 1/L and any small-size persistence misses by about the size ratio. Each rule’s own κ carried to the larger size lands within about 10% (post hoc, geometry-conditional).

    Not a mechanism: "return time grows with scale" is the same statement as "κ is stable" once the dwell is flat. The tail fit failed its calibration, so its exponents are not reported; P1, P3 and P4 came out uninformative, two drifting rules never reached the large sizes, and the pilot outcome was seen before the freeze.

    RLRLLL · A late change of habit

    The column sweeper is checked to run forever

    After roughly 12.7 billion steps on a blank grid, RLRLLL switches to sweeping up and down, laying columns with a three-colour wake. Every one of its 4,729 cycles adds exactly two rows and takes exactly 12m + 934, 1058 or 898 steps. A separate checker shows that the three-phase pattern reproduces itself for every column height, so the sweeper never stops, and |x|²/t tends to 3/4, not the fitted 0.86. A second checker, written separately in Python, reproduces every constant, and the two lemmas that carry the induction are now machine-checked in Lean for every column height. Lean now also checks the end runs, their composition for every even height, and each complete cycle with its exact clock and first arrival at the new front.

    The check still rests on one long simulation that the second checker did not repeat, and the complete-cycle Lean theorem still needs a padding argument before it can be iterated forever: each cycle consumes part of its blank boundary. Re-examining the starting point showed that our first safety margin (three rows around the sweeper) was too small; an exact test, that the real board equals the ideal sweeper on every cell it will ever read, passes in all 50 windows. It certifies the sweeper without explaining it, and what triggers the switch inside the blob is still unknown.

    Opening turns · Edge entry

    A short run of turns predicts the cost of fresh ground

    The opening same-letter run predicts births per boundary step: 1/c ≈ 1.36 + 0.70m. It transfers to fresh stationary rules and survives changing the initial interior colours in flat-band tests.

    Broader validation misses its accuracy target. The apparent effect on occupation at fixed area can arise from conditioning on area; the constants remain unexplained.

    Edge entry · A ceiling, and a failed blueprint

    Chaotic ants with the crawler’s turn table stop just short of its cost

    The square-ring crawler spends exactly k + 5/4 boundary reads per new cell, which the edge tracer reproduces to 0.002 on all 25 board fillers. Chaotic blobs that carry the same turn table sit about 65% of the way from the lead law to that line, against 15% for rules with the same letters in other orders, and only 6 of 129 such rules go above it. The letter-by-letter story failed: the important letters do not shift outward as the opening run grows, and adding one crawler letter usually lowers the cost.

    One blank start per rule, horizon 224. Two thirds of fresh crawler-table rules began filling and were filtered out, so the measured rules are selected. A support-specific steeper slope failed against composition-matched controls, and an earlier fitted kernel already predicts these rules for leads up to 4.

    Other grids · Edge entry

    The edge-entry slope falls as the ant’s loop gets longer

    We moved the ant to hexagonal and triangular cells and wrote down three guesses for the slope of 1/c against the opening run before measuring. All three failed: the slope is 0.86 for a 3-cell loop, 0.70 on squares (4), and about 0.3 for 6-cell loops. Ants also sit below the uniform reference on every grid (0.74 to 0.86), so that deficit is not a feature of squares.

    The falling-slope pattern is a post hoc reading of four strata; one hex fit was ruined by an outlier rule, and there is no mechanism yet.

    Other grids · Where the slope lives

    The registered colour decomposition failed; the slope sits in boundary reads

    We predicted how the 1/c lead slope splits across the colours the ant circulates through, and three of the registered predictions failed (a fourth passed only on an estimator contaminated by interior cells, so it does not count). Looking again after the fact, the slope sits in boundary reads at the circulating colours: about 0.95 per colour on hex R, 0.8 on squares, 0.65 to 0.35 on triangles. Returns after a full ring carry it only on the square grid; on hex r the ant fills ring centres (15% of lead-1 births).

    The per-colour numbers are post hoc. What sets the survival plateau of each colour is open.

    Read the colour test ↗ Predictions, results and provenance

    Boundary use · Where the spread comes from

    The first eight letters predict boundary use, but only as a family label

    Across 2185 stationary rules, the first eight letters of a rule predict about half the spread in boundary occupation (held-out R² 0.62 on 162 fresh rules); the last eight and the letter counts do not. The opening run alone predicts nothing, against my own prediction. When whole six-letter openings are held out, every model falls below the training mean (lookup 0.59 to −0.16).

    The signal is family membership, not a letter-level rule that transfers. The variance shares are post hoc and rough to about ±0.1; about a third of the rule-determined spread is unexplained.

    What we’re working on

    26 October 2026

    Those results narrow to one main question: what makes boundary access settle into the scaling needed for cube-root growth, and when does it fail? The sequence below remains the plan.

    The hidden-phase pilot reported above is complete and stopped at its failed support gate.

    Why we are narrowing the focus ↗

    Instrument update: on one M4 Max desktop, the Metal census runner was 2.01× faster than the best CPU runner for 4083 rules at up to ten million steps with a 1 GiB tile pool. The default pool forced retries and was 2.00× slower; small batches also favoured CPU. Five repetitions per setting, identical CSVs, active desktop applications. Experiment page · Timing settings, ranges and evidence.

    How the turn on fresh ground shapes the edge

    New since 26 October: randomising the turn on first departure disrupts the compact regime in the six rules tested, and a memoryless walker given only the forced turn gets most of the way there over finite windows. Registered but not yet run: forced L on first departure, excursion statistics as a direct test of the reflecting-edge story, a longer convergence run, and fresh-only versus colour-zero noise; proposed, not registered: where between occasional and constant leaks the edge fails, and whether the ant’s memory adds rotor-like balancing. A boundary-access explanation should now say how the fresh-ground turn shapes returns.

    Wall or memory ↗ Q30 and Q31

    Choose a test that can separate explanations

    The transport claim ledger now separates observations, failed explanations and instrument gaps. The older gate sampler’s baseline eligibility remains unknown. The new hidden-phase pilot accounts for every baseline but has too few long episodes for confirmation. Next, choose two explanations with different observable predictions: the model must predict later boundary occupation or drift on separate trajectories and beat persistence with the same inputs. Calibration and an excluded pilot come before one confirmation study.

    Proof progress and remaining gaps ↗ What a mechanism must establish

    Where on the edge does the ant go missing?

    Completed side test, registered and run: split boundary time by how exposed each edge cell is, using a ratio that is exactly 1 for a uniform walker and needs no 1.11 reference. The deficit survived (median ant 0.87, rotor 1.80, random walk 1.31), and κ/1.105 matched it rule by rule to within roughly ±7–9%. The prediction that the ant favours flat edge over corners and tips failed: only 64% of rules agree (95% interval 52–75%), and the rotor and random walk show the same tilt. A review on 25 October found that the after-the-fact flat-edge reading is mostly class composition: compared with the rotor, the ant is short on flat and exposed edge alike, and about half the gap is birth steps. This test ran on 20 October, before the narrowed plan above, and is not a step in that sequence. A follow-up would need criteria at fixed class weights, and must also record each edge cell’s colour phase, because the hidden-phase pilot’s checked example shows the next turn alone does not fix a return path (that pilot failed its support gate).
